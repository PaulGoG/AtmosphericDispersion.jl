Validation

The solver is checked against the analytic properties of the Gaussian plume. These are the statements that fail first if a normalisation, a reflection term or a sector width is wrong.

CheckResult
Crosswind integral equals 2exp(−H²/2Σ_z²)/(√(2π) Σ_z u)exact to 1 part in 10⁸
Mass conservation, u ∫∫ χ/Q dy dz = 11.000000000
Sector average equals the crosswind integral over the arcexact to 1 part in 10¹⁰
Ground-level maximum at Σ_z = H/√2exact under its own assumptions

Mass conservation is the strongest of them: the released activity crossing any downwind plane, carried at the transport speed, is the whole release, so it exercises the 2πΣyΣz normalisation, the ground reflection and the transport speed together.

Against the published literature

The governing normative is CNCAN NSR-23 (Comisia Națională pentru Controlul Activităților Nucleare, 2004), the Romanian nuclear regulator's norm the 2021 thesis was written against. Every parameterisation it prescribes is a standard published scheme, and where the two disagree this package follows the international source and keeps the normative's value available. Where they agree, the identity is asserted.

Four dispersion schemes, and their range

The normative's pairing (Briggs' open-country σy with Hosker's σz) is one of four schemes a Site can evaluate, selected by [model] dispersion:

Briggs' two sets are Briggs (1973) Appendix D, which tabulates plume half-widths R = 1.25 σ for rural and urban sites, as the Handbook on Atmospheric Diffusion (Hanna et al., 1982) Table 4.5 prints them: the σ themselves, rounded to the figures shown, and quoted for 10² < x < 10⁴ m. The urban set is Briggs' fit to the St. Louis experiment of McElroy and Pooler (1968); classes A and B share a curve, as do E and F. The Handbook prints the denominator of the E–F urban σ_z as (1 + 0.00015x); Briggs' own table and EPA ISC3 (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 1995) Table 1-4 have 0.0015, and 0.0015 is what the package carries. The suite asserts the Handbook's printing exactly, that one coefficient excepted, and Briggs' R/1.25 to the rounding, which reaches 7 % where 0.056 became 0.06.

The Eimutis–Konicek scheme is the analytic fit of Eimutis and Konicek (1972) to the Pasquill–Gifford curves, σ_y = a x^0.9031 and σ_z = a x^b + c on three ranges of distance, which the NRC codes XOQDOQ (Sagendorf et al., 1982) and PAVAN (Bander, 1982) evaluate. The paper is not open; the 42 coefficients are those of the two codes' DATA statements, which are identical, and each is asserted individually. The three pieces of each class join to within a per cent at 100 m and 1 km. Both codes limit σ_y and σ_z to 1000 m in subroutine POLYN, "a conservative mixing limit", and the package does the same; without the limit the class A fit gives a σ_z of 4.6 km at 3 km.

Every scheme states the range of distance it was fitted over, and the package checks runs against it. validity_range returns the band; a configuration whose receptor grid reaches outside it is loaded with a warning, refused, or accepted silently as [model] extrapolation directs, and scripts/run.jl reports how many radii extrapolate. The reference configuration's 20 km grid is twice the default scheme's band (Gaussian screening to 20 km is ordinary regulatory practice, SRS-19 stopping there), and the file sets allow. The four schemes for class D, across their bands, are plotted in figures/validation/dispersion_schemes.png.

The vertical dispersion scheme, and two wrong digits

σ_z = g(x)F(z₀,x) is Hosker's analytic fit (Hosker, 1974) to the scheme of F.B. Smith (Smith, 1973), published as IAEA-SM-181/19 in Physical Behaviour of Radioactive Contaminants in the Atmosphere. The normative reproduces it without attribution. It is printed in full in HPA-RPD-058 (Smith and Simmonds, 2009) Table 3.3 and NRPB-R91 (Clarke, 1979) Table 3, and the two printings are identical.

Asserting the whole table against those printings, I found two wrong coefficients in the roughness correction:

z₀NSR-23 Table 2Hoskereffect on σ_z at 100 m
0.01 m, grassland and water1.581.561.9 % too large
0.04 m, arable2.082.023.6 % too large

The effect varies slowly with distance: from 2.9 % at 1 m to 1.4 % at 100 km for the first row, and from 4.2 % to 3.0 % for the second.

The error is the normative's: NSR-23 prints 1.58 and 2.08, and the 2021 code used them as printed. NSR-23 attributes its Table 2 to CAN/CSA-N288.2-M91 and UNSCEAR 2000, so the corruption entered somewhere in that chain rather than at either end. This package follows Hosker.

The other 22 coefficients, and all 24 of the shape function, match exactly in both NSR-23 and Hosker. Nothing else in the suite could have caught it, because the error is in the data rather than the algebra.

The long-term equation is a regulatory one

dilution_long_term is a standard regulatory form: the same equation, constant included, is stated by NRC Regulatory Guide 1.111 Rev. 1 (U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 1977) Eq. (3) and its implementation XOQDOQ (Sagendorf et al., 1982) Eq. (1), by IAEA Safety Reports Series No. 19 (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2001) Eq. (V-2), and by the German AVV zu §47 StrlSchV (Bundesregierung, 2012) Eq. (4.4). RG 1.111 writes the sector constant as 2.032 and says in words that it is √(2/π) divided by a 22.5° sector in radians; SRS-19 works in twelve sectors, where the same constant is 1.5238. Both are asserted, for every class and distance.

dilution_instantaneous is likewise SRS-19 Eq. (V-1) literally.

An end-to-end benchmark

Everything above checks one piece at a time. HPA-RPD-058 Table 3.7 checks them together: "Fractions of material remaining in the plume due to dry deposition for a deposition velocity of 10⁻² m s⁻¹", three effective release heights × seven stability categories × eight distances out to 100 km, with the stack-height wind speed printed for each row. The six categories the package has, A to F, give 144 cells.

Reproducing it exercises σ_z, the mixing layer and the depletion integral at once. 143 of the 144 cells agree to within 0.028, the table's own printed precision being 0.01, and every row is monotone.

In the one exception, category F at 30 m, the table reads 0.13 at 50 km and 0.19 at 100 km, a depletion factor that rises with distance; this is impossible, since material already deposited does not come back. This package gives 0.0188 there, which is monotone and one decimal point from the printed 0.19. The test leaves that cell out and pins the computed value instead.

That benchmark also fixes what a lid does to a source standing at it. Table 3.7 tabulates a 100 m release in category F, whose mixing depth is 100 m, and only the capped form reproduces it: 0.356 and 0.089 at 50 and 100 km against a published 0.33 and 0.083, where the uncapped form gives 0.599 and 0.311. What happens to a plume above the lid is a separate matter, and one of convention; see the mixing layer below.

Published benchmarks state the effective release height and the transport wind speed as inputs, where the package derives both. To reproduce them, PrescribedPlume wraps a Site and takes those inputs as given (the two σ as well, for Turner's problems), bypassing plume rise, the wind profile and the dispersion curves. A prescribed σ_z is valid at one distance only, so the depletion integral rejects it.

The screening tables of SRS-19, and XOQDOQ's own test case

Two further published calculations run through the package end to end.

IAEA Safety Reports Series No. 19 (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2001) tabulates its 30°-sector diffusion factor F (Eq. (3), Table I) for seven bands of release height and its wake-corrected factor B (Eqs. (4)–(6), Table II) for ten bands of building area, both to one significant figure at eleven distances from 100 m to 20 km, and works three examples on them in Annex IV. The σz behind the tables are stated in the notes to Table I: Briggs' open-country class D form up to 45 m, which the `briggsopencountryscheme evaluates, and the Jülich and Karlsruhe power lawsE x^G` above it, which enter as a prescribed σz. SRS-19's screening rule holds F at its maximum over distance for every receptor nearer than that maximum (its Fig. 8). Read through that rule, all 77 cells of Table I are reproduced to the printed figure by a release height inside the band, and 106 of the 110 cells of Table II at the lower edge of the area band. The four that are not sit on a rounding boundary: 7.5 × 10⁻⁴ printed as 8, and 3.5 × 10⁻⁷ or below printed as 4 where the table repeats Table I's last row across every band, the wake term being a 3 % effect at 20 km. Every cell lies within the printed figure's half-unit widened by a tenth.

The worked examples exercise the zones SRS-19 §3.3 draws around a building, which building_zone classifies (the displacement zone above 2.5 H_B, the wake zone beyond 2.5 √A_B, and the cavity between), and the two cavity forms of §3.6: B₀/(u x²) with B₀ = 30 for a receptor on the same wall as the vent, dilution_cavity_wall, after Wilson and Britter (1982), and 1/(π u H_B K) with K = 1 m for one that is not, dilution_cavity, the empirical form of Miller and Yildiran (1984) that SRS-19 qualifies with the width rule of Huber (1984). Example IV-1's farm reads F = 10⁻⁵ m⁻² from Table I, and the package gives 1.3 × 10⁻⁵; IV-2's intake on the wall comes out at exactly the report's 0.6 Bq/m³ and its farm at B = 4 × 10⁻⁵; IV-3's residence in the cavity at 1.3 × 10⁻³ Bq/m³.

NRC XOQDOQ Test Case 2 (Sagendorf et al., 1982) is the code's own continuous elevated release, Appendices B and C: a 10 m/s jet from a 2 m stack at 45 m with no heat, a joint frequency distribution of 100 hours in three directions over five wind-speed classes and stability classes C to G, terrain rising to 200 m at 10 km, and the undepleted annual-average χ/Q printed at 22 distances from 0.25 to 50 miles to four figures. Everything the code does on the way is stated in the report and reproduced from it: the wind adjusted to the release height by a power law with exponent 0.25 or 0.5 by class, Briggs' momentum rise with Gifford's downwash and the exponents as the FORTRAN rounds them, the terrain interpolated linearly with the effective height floored at zero, and the Eimutis–Konicek σ capped at 1000 m and class G taken as σ_F²/σ_E. The sector-averaged kernel with its 2.032 is the package's own, dilution_extended over a plume prescribed cell by cell. All 22 values agree to within 0.05 %, the rounding of the printed figures, and the sector receiving half the hours prints exactly twice them.

The mixing layer

A plume does not disperse upwards forever. Turbulent mixing is capped by an inversion, and once σ_z approaches that depth the plume is trapped between the ground and the lid and reflects off both. Without the lid, agreement with published depletion tables in the far field is limited.

The lid model is HPA-RPD-058 §3.2.2.1 Eq. (3.4), the image sum over virtual sources at 2sA ± h_e. The report truncates it at |s| = 1 and changes to the uniform profile 1/A of Eq. (3.5) once σz reaches the depth, noting that the series "can be summed to any prescribed accuracy". The package sums it: the image series while `Σz < 0.7 A, and above that the cosine series of the same function, whose leading term is Eq. (3.5). There is no change of formula, the activity between the ground and the lid integrates to one to within 4 × 10⁻¹⁶ at every σ_z, and the profile tends to1/A: 1.4 % above it atΣz = A, 5 × 10⁻⁹ at2A. The truncated sum stays within 0.06 % of the converged one up toΣz = Aand is 3 % low at1.5 A`.

Depths are Table 3.5(a), which the report attributes to Clarke (1979) and Jones (1980):

ABCDEF

The depths are greater in unstable air and smaller in stable air, which is the physical ordering. The depth is a site measurement where there is one, so MixingLayer takes any six depths. In a configuration, [mixing_layer] scheme selects tabulated (the default), uniform with a uniform_depth (HPA-RPD-058 §3.2.2.2.4 reports the recommendation of 800 m "for all conditions" for long-range dispersion, where all releases are taken in neutral stability), custom with six depths, or unbounded.

The unstable classes reach the lid first, despite having the deepest one, because σz grows fastest there. For a plume below the lid the correction can only raise the ground-level concentration, since the lid reflects back down material that would otherwise have kept rising; it is therefore in the conservative direction. A plume above the lid under `fullpenetration`, below, is the exception.

A plume above the lid. Class F is a different case: its tabulated depth is 100 m while plume rise puts the effective release at 100.8 m. Published practice treats a plume above the lid in two ways, and the package carries both, as above_lid:

  • rise_inhibited, the default. NRPB-R157 (Jones, 1983) §B2.3: "plume rise will be inhibited by a capping inversion to the mixing layer. If a plume rises into such an inversion the amount of material in the mixing layer, and hence ground-level concentration, will be reduced." NRPB-R157 does not say by how much. Holding the whole plume at the lid, H = min(H, A), is the upper bound of that statement and the package's own choice. It is continuous in H and joins the case Table 3.7 tabulates, a source at the height of the lid. It is also what doubles class F in the table above: a source at a reflecting lid is its own image.
  • full_penetration. EPA ISC3, User's Guide vol. II (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 1995) §1.1.6.1: "if the effective stack height, he, exceeds the mixing height, zi, the plume is assumed to fully penetrate the elevated inversion and the ground-level concentration is set equal to zero". ISC3 also takes stable air as unbounded, which is a depth of inf for classes E and F.

Ignoring the lid where H > A, on the argument that such a plume is decoupled from the ground until the inversion breaks (fumigation, which is not modelled here), matches neither published convention and makes the field discontinuous: a plume 0.1 m below a 100 m lid gives twice the ground-level concentration of one 0.1 m above it.

In the reference case the choice moves the undepleted long-term χ/Q at 20 km in the most exposed sector from 4.2 × 10⁻⁹ s m⁻³ with no lid to 5.1 × 10⁻⁹ with the rise inhibited, and to 3.4 × 10⁻⁹ with full penetration.

The analytic invariants (the crosswind integral, the sector average and the Σ_z = H/√2 maximum) are properties of the unbounded Gaussian and are asserted against a site with the lid switched off. They are statements about the kernel's normalisation.

The depletion and resuspension parameters

Each of these constants is traced to a published source below.

Resuspension. K = A exp(−λ₁t) + B exp(−λ₂t) with A = 10⁻⁵ m⁻¹, B = 10⁻⁹ m⁻¹, λ₁ = 10⁻² d⁻¹, λ₂ = 2 × 10⁻⁵ d⁻¹ is IAEA Safety Series No. 57 (International Atomic Energy Agency, 1982), §3.6, Eq. (3.14A): all four constants agree exactly, in the same units, as read in the primary document, which states them in that sentence. It is reference [3] of NSR-23's own bibliography, and the constants reached this package through it. Safety Series 57 is now superseded and says so on every page, but no successor restates these constants. That report also brackets them: A over 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁴ and B over 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻⁸ m⁻¹, with the fast half-life "of the order of weeks" (this gives 69.3 d) and the slow one "in the range 50 to 100 years" (94.9 yr). These brackets are asserted.

A later model, Maxwell and Anspaugh (2011), also in NUREG/CR-7270 (Bixler et al., 2022), supersedes it. It keeps the fast amplitude and puts the fast decay constant at 0.07 d⁻¹ rather than 0.01; its slow term has seven times the amplitude and a hundred times the rate, and it adds a floor of 10⁻⁹ m⁻¹. The default is therefore the higher of the two for the first two and a half years, by a factor of 1.8 at ten days, 6 at thirty and 60 at one year, and the lower from about 905 days. The default is unchanged; [model] resuspension selects the later model.

Washout. The table is NSR-23 Table 7, which the normative attributes to CAN/CSA-N288.2-M91. It has two species rows, selected by WashoutSpecies:

at 1 mm/hrain Λ_Lrain Λ_Hsnow Λ_Lsnow Λ_H

The intensity dependence is the published one: fitting each column in log–log gives an exponent of 0.753 for three of four, and Λ ∝ J^0.75 is Slinn (1977) via NRPB-R322 (Atmospheric Dispersion Modelling Liaison Committee, 2001) §3.1.1. Safety Series 57 §3.4.2 takes a third position, Λ = aI, linear; NRPB-R157 §D3.4 brackets the exponent at 0.5 to 1.0, which contains both, so neither is asserted against the other.

The rain amplitudes bracket every published value found. At 1 mm/h the German AVV Anhang 7 Tabelle 3 gives 7 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ for aerosols and elemental iodine and 3.5 × 10⁻⁵ for tritiated water; Safety Series 57 Table II gives a = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ h(mm·s)⁻¹ for particulates and 1.1 × 10⁻⁴ for elemental iodine, so 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ and 1.1 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ at that rate. All four fall inside both species rows.

The bracketing breaks down in snow, where the normative disagrees with itself. The tritium row's snow values are the rain values divided by 100 and by 500 (600 for the upper value at 5 mm/h), a particle-scavenging suppression that IAEA TECDOC-379 (International Atomic Energy Agency, 1986) §3.5.4 supports for particles (inorganic iodine in powder snow at 0.2 mm/h, 5 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹, against 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ for the same species in rain). But the other-nuclides row runs the other way: its snow values are 20 to 50 times its own rain values, and three to five orders of magnitude above the tritium row's snow values.

Snow scavenging of tritiated water is isotopic exchange at the crystal surface, not impaction, so the suppression is the wrong mechanism for it. Ogram (1985) measured it, and the measurement agrees closely with NSR-23's other-nuclides snow lower limit:

How long washout acts. NSR-23 Eq. (35) writes the wet depletion factor exp(−Λt) with t "the period of time during which the radioactive material is subjected to the process". The 2021 code took t as the duration of the precipitation, the same at every distance. Material scavenged on its way to a receptor is scavenged for its travel time x/u, and only material released while it rains is scavenged at all, so the package takes the share f = min(t_w/T_R, 1) of a release of duration T_R to travel through rain lasting t_w, and the factor becomes 1 − f + f exp(−Λ_L x/u). A precipitation that lasts the whole release gives the familiar exp(−Λ_L x/u); none gives one.

The form is the limit of a precipitation that covers the path for much longer than the travel time. The tests check it against an average over parcels released uniformly through the release, each exposed for the overlap of its transit with a precipitation fixed over the site: with 10⁶ s of precipitation in a 10⁷ s release the two agree to the edge effect, of order (x/u)/t_w. Of the timings that hold the share f of the release, a precipitation that begins with the release leaves the most airborne (one that ends with it, if it outlasts the release); for x/u ≤ t_w ≤ T_R its scavenged share survives better than the form by the relative amount ((exp(Λ x/u) − 1)/Λ − x/u)/t_w. What falls outside the release scavenges nothing of it, so the condition is min(t_w, T_R) ≫ (exp(Λ x/u) − 1)/Λ, which reduces to min(t_w, T_R) ≫ x/u only while Λ x/u is small. A shorter precipitation has no single answer, because the result depends on when it falls. For Λ_L = 10⁻³ s⁻¹, 900 s of it in a 3600 s release and x/u = 3333 s, the surviving fraction is 0.92 if it falls at the start of the release and 0.47 at the end, and the form gives 0.76. Capping the exposure at t_w is no bound either (0.85 in that case). Omitting washout bounds the airborne concentration for any timing, as EPA ISC3 (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 1995) §1.4 and IAEA SRS-19 (International Atomic Energy Agency, 2001) §3.8 note. The package keeps the form, and load_configuration warns when, at the edge of the receptor grid in the slowest class, the precipitation so timed would leave more than 10 % more airborne than the form. An hour of rain on HTO through a one-hour release changes it by 2.5 % at 20 km; ten hours of snow at 5 mm/h on the other species through a ten-hour release leave 0.040 airborne against 0.014.

Wet deposition, NSR-23 Eq. (38), is the high coefficient times the whole release depleted with the low one over the duration of the event. It does not vanish without precipitation, and one hour of rain at 5 mm/h deposited 1.8 times the whole release within 20 km, however long the release. The package takes the share f instead, and does not deplete the column on its way to the receptor: ω_w = Λ_H f A D exp(−y²/2Σ_y²)/(√(2π) Σ_y u). Across the plume that is Λ_H f A D/u per metre, the largest deposit a precipitation of duration t_w can lay at that distance, whenever and wherever it falls, since no more than f A passes while it falls and each becquerel deposits Λ_H/u per metre. A shower over the receptor alone deposits that much when nothing else depletes the plume, spread across it with the meander of min(t_w, T_R), which the release_duration keyword sets. Precipitation over the whole path depletes the plume first, to exp(−Λ_H x/u) of that value if it lasts long, and one that begins over a plume already airborne lies in between. Where Λ_H x/u ≫ 1 the onset dominates: for snow on the other species at 1 mm/h (Λ_H = 2 × 10⁻² s⁻¹), 20 km downwind at 6 m/s, the depleted form falls short of it by a factor of order 10²⁴, which is why the package does not deplete. The tests check the bound against Lagrangian deposits over 41 timings, for precipitation over the whole path and over the receptor alone. Each receptor takes its own worst case, so the deposit integrated over the ground, f A Λ_H X/u out to X before decay, exceeds the activity released into the precipitation beyond X = u/Λ_H; ISC3 §1.4 likewise calls a deposition computed without wet depletion conservative.

The tritium deposition velocities

The package uses the deposition velocities in the notes to NSR-23 Table 6, which state that of HTO as 0.4–0.8 × 10⁻² m/s and that of HT as an order of magnitude lower, 0.04–0.05 × 10⁻² m/s, both attributed to experimental measurement by Murphy (1993), under the stated condition that the tropopause be taken at 12–15 km.

The lower bound is close to measured values. Lee et al. (2012) report a daylight average of about 0.42 cm/s over forest at the Savannah River Site, within a published range of 0.07 to 2.8 cm/s, and 0.5 cm/s was the value of the MACCS2 guidance their report re-examines.

A deposition velocity above zero for HTO is a deliberate divergence, and it is not the conservative choice for the air concentration. IAEA SRS-19 §3.9, EUR 15760 (Simmonds et al., 1995) §3.2 and HPA-RPD-058 §3.2.2.3 each assign tritium a deposition velocity of zero, handling it by specific activity instead, and Lee et al. (2012) conclude that, because deposited HTO is re-emitted, an effective velocity of zero is appropriate for safety analyses. This package follows the normative the work was done under; setting both velocities to zero follows the others.

Where schemes disagree, the choice is in the configuration

Three plume-rise constants differ between published schemes, and the 2021 code took a position on each without recording one. Here they form a RiseCoefficients value selected by [model] plume_rise, defaulting to Briggs:

Combined-law buoyancy denominator0.72 = 2β², β = 0.60.5—
Neutral momentum rise, c w₀D/u3: Briggs (1969) Eq. 5.2, EPA ISC3 Eq. (1-16)1.5, the momentum term of Holland (1953), Turner (1970) Eq. (4.1)—

The first is the one that matters. The combined momentum-and-buoyancy law must reduce to the pure-buoyancy law when the momentum flux vanishes, and with 0.5 it did not: it left (6F x²/u³)^(1/3) against the two-thirds law's (1.6³F x²/u³)^(1/3), an overshoot of 13.6 %. The momentum half of the same expression already had the published form (β_j = 1/3 + u/w₀, EPA ISC3 Eq. (1-27); the Handbook has 0.4 + 1.2u/w₀), which placed the discrepancy in the buoyancy half alone, and two independent routes give the correction: 2β² = 0.72 and 3/1.6³ = 0.7324.

With 0.72 the law reduces to within 0.6 %; the residual is that (3/0.72)^(1/3) = 1.60915 where the literature rounds the two-thirds coefficient to 1.6, the same rounding that makes the neutral final rise 21.425 against a published 21.4.

plume_rise = "nsr23" restores the old constants exactly, and the fidelity tests use it to reproduce the 2021 correlations term by term. Two things around them changed in 1.1.0: the stable limits apply in classes E and F only, and inside the balance band the combined law is capped at the larger of the two final rises, where the 2021 code capped it at their sum. NSR-23 §6.3(e) says of the two contributions that they "must not be summed up"; capped at the sum, the far-field rise jumped by nearly a factor of two at the edge of the band.

The reference case does not move: the CANDU stack is buoyancy-dominated (80.2 m of buoyant rise against 11.7 m of momentum rise in class D, an imbalance far outside the tolerance for the combined law), so it takes the pure-buoyancy branch, which none of these constants touch. The correction affects momentum-dominated and balanced releases, the regime of the 13.6 % overshoot.

Selecting xoqdoq moves the stable classes, where the stable branch is active: the effective release height falls from 102.4 m to 98.6 m in class E and from 100.8 m to 97.0 m in class F. The other classes have no stable branch.

The wind of the stable final rise is the mean over the rise. Briggs' stable limit 2.6 [F/(uS)]^(1/3) is Eq. 2.19 of the Handbook, which defines its u as "an average value between the heights hs and hs + Δh". The 2021 code, like XOQDOQ, took the wind at the release height. The rise and the mean depend on each other (a higher rise averages over more of the profile, and a faster mean lowers the rise), so stable_rise_wind_speed solves the two together, iterating from the release-height value until the rise settles to a part in 10¹²; the mean of the power-law profile over a layer is in closed form, layer_mean_wind_speed. The stable limit binds in classes E and F, and there the mean lowers the effective height by about 3 m: class E from 105.0 m to 102.4 m, class F from 103.5 m to 100.8 m, still above its 100 m lid. The long-term χ/Q in the most exposed sector rises by 0.04 % at 1 km, 1.0 % at 2 km, 0.9 % at 5 km and 0.2 % at 20 km. [model] stable_rise_wind = "release_height" restores the release-height convention.

Resuspension is selectable the same way: [model] resuspension takes iaea_ss57 (the default, Safety Series 57) or maxwell_anspaugh (2011, also NUREG/CR-7270).

The building-wake coefficient defaults to 1.0, which is IAEA SRS-19 Eq. (6), Σ_z = (σ_z² + A_B/π)^(1/2), and the German AVV Eqs. (4.31)/(4.32), √(σ² + I_G²/π). NSR-23 Eq. (9) gives C between 0.5 and 2.0 and recommends the value that gives the largest dilution factor; the 2021 code used 1.5, which NSR23_WAKE_COEFFICIENT restores. RG 1.111 Eq. (9) is a third convention again, applying 0.5 to the building height rather than its area.

For a release at ground level a dispersion parameter is widened by at most √3, whatever the coefficient. NRC XOQDOQ, after RG 1.111, bounds the "maximum allowable building-wake dilution" of a ground-level release by 3σ_yσ_z in its point form, Eq. (8), and by √3 σ_z in its sector form, Eq. (11), and NSR-23 §5 recommends, "in case of a discharge at the ground level", that the wake reduce the concentration by a factor "not higher than 3". The limit on each parameter is Eq. (11) exactly and keeps a centreline value within the factor of 3, but where σ_y and σ_z widen unequally it is stricter than the product bound: for σ_y = 100 m, σ_z = 10 m and a 30 000 m² building the reduction is 2.4 where Eq. (8) allows 3, which errs towards the higher concentration. Neither source limits the wake of an elevated plume, and there a limit errs the other way: a wider Σ_z brings the plume down sooner, so for a plume at 50 m in the wake of a 60 m building the limit would lower χ/Q at 200 m by a factor of 9. The package applies it at effective height zero only. SRS-19 sets no limit; [buildings] broadening_limit = inf follows it. The building that sets the wake is the tallest within three of its own heights of the stack, "the building that most influences the resulting plume dispersion" of SRS-19 §3.3, and of equally tall ones the nearest, as NSR-23 §5 takes the nearest building. The 2021 code averaged every building in the list with weights inversely proportional to distance, so a low structure beside the stack could cancel the wake of a tall one.

Snow washout of tritiated water is selectable, as described under the washout table above: [model] washout = "hto" replaces the upper snow coefficient of the tritium row by Ogram's correlation, about a thousand times the normative value; the lower coefficient and the rain values are unchanged. The reference case of this package is HTO, so the choice matters for it.

This code writes the neutral final rise as 1.6F^(1/3)(3.5x*)^(2/3)/u, which does not look like the published 21.4F^(3/4)/u. Substituting x* = 14F^(5/8) reduces it to the published form with the constant 1.6·49^(2/3) = 21.425, which the literature rounds, and 1.6·119^(2/3) = 38.71 for the other branch. The tests assert both the algebra and the numbers.

σz is Hosker's, as above, carrying an explicit roughness correction that Briggs does not have. So the package mixes Briggs σy with Hosker σz, the normative's pairing, which neither author published, and the two are therefore asserted against different sources. Against Briggs, σz is only bracketed: the ratio runs from 0.41 (class B at 10 km) to 1.49 (class E at 10 km) over 0.1–10 km, a factor of 2.4 taken symmetrically, and it is systematically ordered, with less vertical spread than Briggs in unstable air, more in stable and closest in neutral. This is the spread that separates published σ schemes from one another, and figures/validation/dispersion_parameters.png shows it.

Σ_z = H/√2 is derived holding H fixed and Σ_y ∝ Σ_z; asserted under those assumptions it is exact. In the real field the maximum lands 5 % away from it, because Σ_y/Σ_z drifts from 2.07 to 2.72 across the peak region while plume rise has already saturated. All six Pasquill classes and three sector counts are covered.
